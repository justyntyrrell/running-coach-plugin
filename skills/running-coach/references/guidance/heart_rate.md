# Heart-rate drift in prolonged running, heat and hydration, and heart-rate sensor validity

Rendered from the coaching knowledge (version acf61b6f40f3); never edited by hand.

## Rules

### hr-sensor-check

**Guidance:** A key session of the last 14 days had heart rate varying widely across reps at a steady pace (HR_SENSOR_ARTIFACT). Judge it by pace and feel, not heart rate. Wrist optical heart rate was close to ECG on average in treadmill running but varied by brand and exercise type, and an electrode chest strap agreed almost perfectly: suggest a strap for sessions steered by heart rate.

**Purpose:** Keep a sensor artifact from being read as a training response.

**Uncertainty:** The flag is a heuristic, more than 35 beats/min between reps whose pace varied by under 5%, which a real response can also produce. The validation studies are laboratory treadmill tests, not outdoor intervals, and one tested devices since superseded. The context does not say which sensor recorded the session.

**Alternatives:**

- Keep the heart rate when the athlete wore a chest strap and the reps really did differ in effort.

**Claims:** hr-wrist-optical-valid-steady-treadmill, hr-chest-strap-closest-to-ecg.

## Claims

Each with its evidence type, quality, source and link, then what it means in practice.

- **hr-chest-strap-closest-to-ecg** (observational, low quality; Gillinov 2017, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28709155/): Use an electrode chest strap when exact heart rate matters, as the authors advise; threshold tests, intervals, heart-rate-capped runs in the heat and drift analysis are examples (coach inference). A good wrist sensor is a reasonable substitute for steady treadmill-like running.
- **hr-contraception-coc-exercise-hr-unchanged** (meta_analysis, low quality; Fabunmi 2026, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42558369/): Heart-rate zones need no routine correction for a runner on the combined pill: set them from her own tests, as for anyone else. One cohort found a higher submaximal heart rate in pill users, so if her easy-run heart rate looks high, check it against her own baseline.
- **hr-dehydration-3bpm-per-percent-heat** (systematic_review, low quality; Adams 2014, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24736771/): At a fixed intensity in the heat (for a runner, a fixed pace), each 1% of body mass lost went with about 4 beats/min higher post-exercise heart rate, so a 2-3% loss could account for very roughly 8-12 beats/min (coach inference: assumes the figure is linear and applies during the run). Part of the drift on a hot long run can therefore be dehydration rather than lost fitness. Variable-intensity trials showed a smaller rise (1 beat/min per 1%); the abstract does not say whether they were self-paced. Weighing before and after hot long runs separates the two (coach inference).
- **hr-drift-heat-dehydration-relative-intensity** (narrative_review, low quality; Wingo 2015, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25943659/): On a warm or hot run at a steady pace, expect heart rate to climb. In the heat VO2max falls with stroke volume, so the higher heart rate reflects a real rise in relative intensity (%VO2max); Wingo 2020's running study (abstract only) concludes this directly. Slowing to hold a heart-rate cap keeps relative effort steadier, and holding a cool-weather pace raises it (coach inference). Whether the same holds on long temperate runs is not shown.
- **hr-fluid-intake-lowers-exercise-hr** (meta_analysis, low quality; Porto 2023, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37960187/): Drinking water during exercise blunted part of the heart-rate rise compared with drinking nothing (the heart-rate studies are mostly cycling). When reading drift on a run done without fluids, allow that a few beats/min may come from not drinking (coach inference for long or warm runs: the heart-rate studies range from short tests to 2 h or a 48 km mountain-bike race, at about 18-39 C where reported). Do not quote a precise figure, because the paper's overall estimates disagree. Separately, ad libitum drinking performed as well as programmed drinking in 1-2 h runs and rides despite more body-mass loss (Goulet 2019, abstract only; it reports heart rate only as the intensity, not as an outcome), so a lower heart rate should not be read as better performance (coach inference).
- **hr-wrist-optical-valid-steady-treadmill** (meta_analysis, moderate quality; Zhang 2020, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32552580/): On steady treadmill-like running, wrist optical heart rate was unbiased on average across studies, so session averages are a reasonable guide. Mean bias does not show how far one watch or one reading can be off, so check a wrist sensor against a chest strap before relying on it for zones or drift analysis (coach inference). Treat spikes, short segments, cycling and strength sessions with caution.
