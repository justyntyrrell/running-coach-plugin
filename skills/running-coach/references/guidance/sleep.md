# Sleep loss, sleep extension, naps and injury

Rendered from the coaching knowledge (version acf61b6f40f3); never edited by hand.

## Rules

### short-night

**Guidance:** Last night's recorded sleep was 6 hours or less. Before a key session or race, treat it as a real cost to endurance, a few percent and more for long efforts, morning sessions included: judge a long quality session by effort, not pace. An early-afternoon nap of 30-60 minutes, ending an hour or more before training, is a low-cost option.

**Purpose:** Fit the day to a short night instead of chasing paces it makes harder.

**Uncertainty:** 6 hours is where the acute sleep-loss meta-analysis drew sleep loss (6 h or less in 24 h), as its abstract reports. The watch's estimate is not the studies' controlled sleep, the tests were laboratory efforts in mostly male participants, and the consensus, shown as counter-evidence, calls the effect of 1-3 nights of partial restriction unclear, where the protocol meta-analysis found restriction cost about 3%. Sleep need is individual: ask how the athlete feels.

**Alternatives:**

- Keep the plan when the athlete feels fresh and a night this short is their usual.

**Claims:** sleep-loss-impairs-endurance, sleep-loss-endurance-by-protocol, sleep-nap-30-60-min.

**Counter-evidence:** recovery-sleep-individualised.

## Claims

Each with its evidence type, quality, source and link, then what it means in practice.

- **sleep-extension-and-naps-most-effective** (systematic_review, very_low quality; Cunha 2023, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37462808/): For an athlete who habitually sleeps about 7 hours or feels short of sleep, extending time in bed over several nights is the best-supported sleep intervention, with naps a supported alternative mainly for alertness and cognition; generic sleep-hygiene advice alone has little performance evidence. For an athlete already satisfied with their sleep, more time in bed may only add time awake.
- **sleep-injury-not-independent-risk-adults** (systematic_review, low quality; Dobrosielski 2021, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33560506/): Do not present poor sleep as a proven cause of injury; treat it as one context signal beside single-run spikes, pain reports and injury history.
- **sleep-loss-endurance-by-protocol** (meta_analysis, low quality; Craven 2022, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35708888/): Size the cost of a short night as a few percent of endurance performance, with a larger point estimate after no sleep than after partial restriction, though the two were not formally compared, and do not assume an early-morning session or race escapes it.
- **sleep-loss-impairs-endurance** (meta_analysis, low quality; Lopes 2023, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36472094/): Treat a night of very short or no sleep before a key session or race as a real performance cost, larger for long efforts; after such a night, judge a long quality session by effort rather than chase pace targets.
- **sleep-nap-30-60-min** (meta_analysis, low quality; Mesas 2023, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36690376/): After a short night, or before an afternoon or evening session, an early-afternoon nap of 30-60 minutes that ends at least an hour before training is a low-cost option.
- **sleep-quality-runner-injury-cohort** (observational, low quality; Goldberg 2025, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41239840/): Treat a run of poor self-rated sleep, especially with elevated soreness or fatigue, as a prompt to ask about niggles and to hold load steady; it is a warning sign, not a proven cause.
