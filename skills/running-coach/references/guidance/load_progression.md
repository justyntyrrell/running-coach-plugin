# Training-load progression, running injury and return to running

Rendered from the coaching knowledge (version acf61b6f40f3); never edited by hand.

## Rules

### long-run-spike

**Guidance:** A run in the last 7 days was more than 10% longer than the longest run of the 30 days before it. Ask about knee, shin and foot symptoms, and grow the long run in smaller steps from here.

**Purpose:** Single runs well beyond the recent longest are the best-supported load warning for overuse injury.

**Uncertainty:** One large observational cohort. 10% was a category edge chosen in advance, not a proven safe limit, and no trial shows that capping spikes prevents injury.

**Alternatives:**

- Accept the spike without change when it was a planned race or time trial and no symptoms follow.

**Claims:** load-single-session-spike-vs-30day-longest, load-previous-injury-strongest-risk-factor.

### weekly-volume-jump

**Guidance:** The running distance of the last completed week (Monday to Sunday) was at least 30% above the weekly average of the 4 weeks before it. Treat it as a prompt to review symptoms and the size of single runs, not as a hard limit.

**Purpose:** Large weekly increases have a weak, inconsistent link with injury, strongest early in a build.

**Uncertainty:** The 1.3 threshold is a coach choice that refers to the >30% band of Nielsen 2014, whose novices progressed over two weeks and whose record was checked from the abstract only; it is not a limit the evidence sets. The supporting studies are observational, mostly in novice and recreational runners, with imprecise estimates, and the largest cohort (Frandsen 2025, shown as counter-evidence) found no link for week-to-week ratios.

**Alternatives:**

- No change when the jump is a return to usual volume after a planned easy week.

**Claims:** load-weekly-change-evidence-very-limited, load-weekly-jump-over-30pct-distance-injuries, load-halfmarathon-weekly-increase-20-60pct.

**Counter-evidence:** load-single-session-spike-vs-30day-longest.

### return-after-break

**Guidance:** Running resumed after a break of a week or more in the last 28 days. Restore volume before intensity, let symptoms during, after and the day after running set the pace, and treat the pre-break long run as a spike until the recent longest run is back near it. If the break was a diagnosed bone stress injury, its site sets the expected timeline (pooled estimates of about 6 to 8 weeks for the posteromedial tibia or fibula, months for the femoral neck or navicular, a harder return at the anterior tibia), and a past stress fracture is the strongest flag for another.

**Purpose:** After a break the recent longest run falls, so going straight back to earlier distances is a large single-session jump.

**Uncertainty:** Return guidance rests on expert opinion after bone stress injury; nothing tests return after illness or a holiday. Applying the 30-day spike to a return is an inference. The site timelines are pooled estimates for athletes, with wide intervals at some sites; the abstract gives neither the included designs nor how return was defined. The context does not record why the break happened or where an injury was.

**Alternatives:**

- Resume normal training promptly when the break was short, planned and symptom-free.

**Claims:** load-return-after-bone-stress-graded-symptom-led, load-single-session-spike-vs-30day-longest, load-previous-injury-strongest-risk-factor, load-bone-prior-stress-fracture-strongest-predictor, load-bone-return-time-depends-on-site.

### bone-stress-risk-screen

**Guidance:** Load is rising, or running resumed after a break. Women runners have had about twice men's bone stress injury risk, and the risk adds up with Female Athlete Triad factors. Once in a build, and only if the athlete is happy to answer, ask first about any past bone stress injury and where it was. In later chats, one question a chat and only if she is happy to go on, ask whether her periods are regular (on hormonal contraception, how they were before she started), then whether she is eating enough for the training. Suggest a sports physician before load rises further when the Triad consensus's referral points apply (load-bone-triad-cumulative-risk-consensus): periods missing or long infrequent, suspected disordered eating, one high-risk factor or two milder ones, or 6 months or more on a medicine that can affect bone.

**Purpose:** Catch the bone stress injury risk factors that weigh most for women (menstrual disturbance, energy deficiency, a past bone stress injury) at the moments load changes, rather than reading every bony complaint as a training error.

**Uncertainty:** No cited study shows that a weekly jump, a long-run spike or a return raises bone stress injury risk more in women than in men: the load conditions only time the question, at the thresholds weekly-volume-jump, long-run-spike and return-after-break use (coach choices), and the reported sex is the gate. It is silent on race day and for 13 days after a race, while the race is still the spike or still in the completed week the weekly jump compares, also a coach choice. The risk-factor evidence is observational, mostly in collegiate runners. The consensus's Table 5 is its Level B indication for a bone density scan, used here as the point to suggest a sports physician; its referral is wider, on suspected disordered eating or missing or prolonged infrequent periods alone. The coach can only approximate any of these without a clinical history, and menstrual status cannot be read on hormonal contraception.

**Alternatives:**

- Ask once at onboarding and after any bone stress injury, rather than on load triggers.
- Skip when she answered these questions earlier in the same build.
- Skip it when the spike or jump was a planned race or time trial and no symptoms follow, as long-run-spike accepts one.

**Claims:** load-bone-female-runners-higher-bsi-risk, load-bone-triad-cumulative-risk-consensus, load-bone-menstrual-disturbance-more-bsi, load-bone-female-offroad-runner-risk-factors, load-bone-prior-stress-fracture-strongest-predictor, fuel-low-energy-availability-reds.

### no-fixed-load-rules

**Guidance:** Do not present a 10%-per-week limit or an acute:chronic workload ratio zone as an injury rule. Judge load from single-run spikes, symptoms and the athlete's injury history.

**Purpose:** Neither rule is supported: the 10% rule failed in a trial, and ACWR associations run in both directions and carry statistical artefacts.

**Uncertainty:** Absence of support is not proof of no effect; large weekly jumps may still matter for some runners.

**Alternatives:**


**Claims:** load-ten-percent-rule-not-protective, load-acwr-not-a-valid-management-target, load-acwr-inverse-in-runners, load-acwr-high-days-marathon-amateurs, load-weekly-change-evidence-very-limited.

## Claims

Each with its evidence type, quality, source and link, then what it means in practice.

- **load-acwr-high-days-marathon-amateurs** (observational, low quality; Toresdahl 2023, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36113976/): A last-7-days volume well above the 4-week average (about 1.5x) can prompt a review, not a hard cutoff.
- **load-acwr-inverse-in-runners** (observational, low quality; Nakaoka 2021, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34052983/): Do not penalise a recent load increase for a high ratio alone; a sharp load drop may signal a niggle, so ask about symptoms.
- **load-acwr-not-a-valid-management-target** (narrative_review, moderate quality; Impellizzeri 2020, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32502973/): Do not use ACWR sweet spots or danger zones as injury-prevention rules; at most show the ratio as a descriptive trend.
- **load-bone-female-offroad-runner-risk-factors** (systematic_review, low quality; Stennett 2025, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39874957/): With a female trail or cross-country runner, screen the changeable factors (menstrual regularity, energy, calcium and vitamin D intake) together with any past bone stress injury, and refer for assessment when several are present.
- **load-bone-female-runners-higher-bsi-risk** (meta_analysis, moderate quality; Hollander 2021, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33433864/): For a female runner, treat bone stress injury, not overuse injury in general, as the injury more common in women: take focal bony pain seriously early and ask about the known bone stress injury risk factors (prior bone stress injury, menstrual regularity, energy intake).
- **load-bone-menstrual-disturbance-more-bsi** (observational, low quality; Hutson 2021, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34269142/): Ask a female runner whether her periods are regular. Fewer than nine a year is a reason to suspect low energy availability and to refer for medical assessment before adding load; plyometrics are not a substitute for fixing it.
- **load-bone-prior-stress-fracture-strongest-predictor** (meta_analysis, moderate quality; Wright 2015, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26582192/): Treat a past stress fracture as the strongest single flag for another (a past stress reaction is a reasonable extension, not tested here): ask about it at setup and on any return, and build volume and impact more conservatively after one.
- **load-bone-return-time-depends-on-site** (meta_analysis, low quality; Hoenig 2023, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36720584/): After a diagnosed bone stress injury, let the site the clinician names set the expected timeline: months for the femoral neck or navicular, about 6 to 8 weeks for the posteromedial tibia or fibula, and a harder return with more complications at the anterior tibia. These are pooled estimates with wide intervals, a guide rather than a schedule. Do not run every return on one template.
- **load-bone-triad-cumulative-risk-consensus** (consensus, moderate quality; Williams 2026, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41474492/): The consensus's Level B guideline (Table 5) calls for a bone density (DXA) scan in an adult athlete with any one of these at its severe end (no period for over 3 months or fewer than 6 a year, a first period at 15 or later, very low body weight or rapid weight loss, a diagnosed eating disorder, a past scan showing low bone density or osteoporosis, a bone stress injury at a high-risk site such as the femoral neck, navicular or anterior tibia, or two or more past bone stress injuries), with two or more milder ones (6-9 periods a year, restrictive eating, a first period at 14, mildly low body weight or moderate recent weight loss, one past low-risk bone stress injury), or after 6 months or more on a medicine that can affect bone (the contraceptive injection, glucocorticoid tablets, GnRH analogues): refer her to a sports physician before raising load. Suspected disordered eating on its own calls for evaluation by a physician, a dietitian and a mental-health professional (Level B), and missing or prolonged infrequent periods for a physician's evaluation, so refer on those alone too. On hormonal contraception, ask about her periods before she started. After an injury, expect clearance to weigh that risk alongside the injury's site and grade.
- **load-contraception-hc-training-response-unchanged** (observational, low quality; O'Leary 2025, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39501462/): Do not expect contraceptive type (combined, progestin-only or none) to change how much a training block improves her running: this cohort found no difference, so plan load and progression the same way.
- **load-detraining-front-loaded-after-stop** (observational, low quality; Coyle 1984, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6511559/): After a full stop, past fitness is lost fastest in the first 3 weeks and the loss levels off by about 8: weeks without running should cost an Athlete's past volume its weight quickly at first, then less.
- **load-halfmarathon-weekly-increase-20-60pct** (observational, low quality; Damsted 2019, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30526231/): Be most careful early in a half-marathon block and avoid stacking large weekly increases there; one big week is a weak predictor on its own.
- **load-postpartum-activity-spares-milk-and-infant-growth** (meta_analysis, low quality; Jones 2025, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39375006/): Breastfeeding is not shown to be a reason on its own to delay a postpartum return to running, though the abstract gives no exercise doses, so this is not shown at running volumes. Concerns about milk supply belong with her clinician or lactation adviser.
- **load-postpartum-elite-early-return-injury-signal** (meta_analysis, very_low quality; Kimber 2021, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33560776/): Elite athletes may return before 6 weeks more often than non-elite athletes (very low certainty), but that is not shown to be safe, so an elite timeline is not a target. Referring a postpartum runner with new bone pain is coach inference; stress fractures were among the injuries reported.
- **load-postpartum-pelvic-floor-training-cuts-incontinence** (meta_analysis, moderate quality; Beamish 2025, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39694630/): Encourage pelvic floor muscle training through the first postpartum year. Referring a runner who leaks or feels pelvic heaviness while running to a pelvic health clinician is coach inference, not from this source.
- **load-postpartum-pregnancy-vigorous-exercise-third-trimester** (meta_analysis, low quality; Beetham 2019, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31391016/): For a pregnant runner whose clinician has cleared exercise, continuing vigorous exercise, running included, into the third trimester is not shown to harm the baby's growth, though one study the review did not pool links daily vigorous exercise early in pregnancy to small babies. The authors call for research above 90% of maximum heart rate, so this review gives such efforts no cover.
- **load-postpartum-return-run-programme-delphi** (consensus, very_low quality; Deering 2024, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38191239/): For a postpartum runner, start with walk-run sessions, raise duration and intensity gradually, keep strength work in the week, and change the plan when she reports pelvic or musculoskeletal symptoms, poor sleep, low mood, or lactation or fuelling concerns.
- **load-postpartum-return-run-readiness-delphi** (consensus, very_low quality; Christopher 2024, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38148108/): When an athlete says she has recently given birth, do not plan running in the first 3 weeks or on a fixed calendar date. Treat the first run as an individual decision after screening by her clinician, and build gradually from it.
- **load-previous-injury-strongest-risk-factor** (systematic_review, moderate quality; Hulme 2017, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27785775/): Weigh the athlete's injury history above any volume rule, and progress more conservatively after recent or recurring injury.
- **load-reduced-training-keeps-endurance** (observational, low quality; Hickson 1982, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6214534/): A block run at two thirds or more of an Athlete's usual km, with intensity kept (a taper, a recovery block), is a reduction that keeps what they built, not a fresh start: their past volume stays evidence. Much less than that starts to cost long endurance.
- **load-return-after-bone-stress-graded-symptom-led** (scoping_review, very_low quality; George 2024, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39141251/): After an injury layoff, reintroduce running in stages, restore volume before intensity, and let symptoms during, after and the day after running set the pace; present it as expert consensus.
- **load-single-session-spike-vs-30day-longest** (observational, moderate quality; Frandsen 2025, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40623829/): Compare each run's distance with the longest run of the previous 30 days and grow the long run in small steps. 10% was a pre-set category edge, not a proven safe limit.
- **load-ten-percent-rule-not-protective** (rct, moderate quality; Buist 2008, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17940147/): Do not present 10% per week as a safety rule. At most it is a soft default for pacing a build; watch single-session spikes, injury history and symptoms instead.
- **load-weekly-change-evidence-very-limited** (systematic_review, low quality; Damsted 2018, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30534459/): Treat any weekly-progression limit as a low-certainty heuristic that athlete history and response can override.
- **load-weekly-jump-over-30pct-distance-injuries** (observational, low quality; Nielsen 2014, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25155475/): A sustained jump above ~30% a week is a soft warning sign, especially for knee, iliotibial band and shin complaints, not a proven cutoff.
