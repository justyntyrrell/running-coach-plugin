# Fueling, race nutrition, energy availability and iron

Rendered from the coaching knowledge (version acf61b6f40f3); never edited by hand.

## Rules

### race-fueling-rehearsal

**Guidance:** Settle the race fuelling plan now and rehearse it in long runs and race-pace sessions: carbohydrate-rich eating before the race, in-race carbohydrate scaled to expected duration (little or none for about an hour, high hourly intake for a marathon), and caffeine only at a dose already tried in training.

**Purpose:** Carbohydrate availability and a trained gut protect late-race pace and limit gastrointestinal trouble.

**Uncertainty:** Benefits grow with event duration and are smaller in trained athletes; most trials are laboratory tests. Gut training timelines are unknown.

**Alternatives:**

- For a half marathon around an hour, a pre-race meal and a mouth rinse or a single gel instead of a full in-race plan.

**Claims:** fuel-race-carbohydrate-scales-with-duration, fuel-carbohydrate-during-exercise-helps, fuel-train-the-gut, fuel-caffeine-3-6-mg-kg.

### illness-energy-intake

**Guidance:** Illness in a high-volume runner, like unexplained fatigue or falling performance, is a prompt to ask about energy intake (relative energy deficiency in sport), not only about load.

**Purpose:** Catch low energy availability, which harms health and performance, before it is read as a training problem alone.

**Uncertainty:** The consensus sets no volume: how high is the coach's call, from the athlete's own weeks. Energy availability is hard to measure in practice, so the coach asks rather than estimates it. Low-energy markers do not always come with a performance loss over a few weeks (Kuikman 2022), so falling performance alone does not show under-fuelling.

**Alternatives:**

- Treat a single cold in an ordinary week as an illness question only.

**Claims:** fuel-low-energy-availability-reds, fuel-lea-common-in-athletes.

**Counter-evidence:** fuel-lea-markers-not-obligatory-underperformance.

### illness-cycle-energy-check

**Guidance:** If the athlete is happy to talk about it, ask first whether her periods have become irregular or stopped; on a combined pill, whose bleeds are withdrawal bleeds, ask instead how her periods were before she started. In a later chat, and only if she is happy to go on, ask whether she has lost weight without meaning to, or on a combined pill about any bone stress injury. Read a change as a sign of energy deficiency and bone-stress risk, not a convenience, and suggest a medical review; the tested remedy is a moderate, sustained increase in food under care, not a calorie target from the coach.

**Purpose:** Use the menstrual cycle, the most practical marker an amateur has, to catch energy deficiency and bone-stress risk before an injury.

**Uncertainty:** No claim sets a training volume. The cycle is no marker after menopause or while hormonal contraception sets the bleeding pattern, and the consensus grades the risk of energy-related menstrual change as falling with gynecological age. Illness is the trigger because Whitney 2025 linked it to low-energy indicators in women marathoners (unadjusted); it is not specific, and the reported sex is a coach choice of gate.

**Alternatives:**

- Ask once in a build instead of on illness.
- Treat a single cold in an ordinary week as an illness question only.

**Claims:** fuel-triad-factors-raise-bone-stress-risk, fuel-menstrual-disturbance-dose-not-threshold, fuel-lea-indicators-slower-marathon-women, fuel-more-food-restores-menses, fuel-contraception-coc-masks-energy-deficiency.

### iron-status-check

**Guidance:** Ask whether the athlete knows her recent ferritin and haemoglobin. If they have not been checked recently, or she reports unusual fatigue or stalled endurance despite consistent training, suggest a blood test through her clinician. Do not suggest taking iron without a result: the benefit depends on how low stores are, and oral iron often upsets the gut.

**Purpose:** Low iron stores are common in women athletes and go with poorer endurance; iron raises stores when they are low, but the gain depends on the starting level and comes with gut side effects.

**Uncertainty:** No cited claim tests screening itself: no trial shows that checking ferritin in women runners improves performance or health. The prevalence figure comes from one elite cohort and a 35 ng/mL cut-off; the endurance cost of deficiency comes from small studies; and whether iron improves performance, not just iron status, is contested (in active women, most trials that measured VO2max or lactate found no significant change, and in female athletes the time-to-exhaustion gains at 100 mg doses did not differ significantly from placebo). The female gate and the build window are coach choices: men become iron deficient too, less often.

**Alternatives:**

- Ask only when she reports fatigue or a performance plateau, not in every build.
- Leave iron entirely to her clinician and discuss it only when she raises it.

**Claims:** fuel-iron-low-ferritin-half-of-elite-women, fuel-iron-female-athlete-deficiency-endurance-cost, fuel-iron-oral-response-depends-on-baseline-ferritin, fuel-iron-supplement-vo2max-women-reproductive-age, fuel-iron-daily-supplement-menstruating-women-gi-cost.

**Counter-evidence:** fuel-iron-ferrous-sulfate-raises-stores-active-women.

## Claims

Each with its evidence type, quality, source and link, then what it means in practice.

- **fuel-caffeine-3-6-mg-kg** (consensus, moderate quality; Guest 2021, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33388079/): Caffeine is an option for the race if it has been tried in training at a modest dose; weigh sleep and gut effects.
- **fuel-carbohydrate-during-exercise-helps** (meta_analysis, moderate quality; Ramos-Campo 2024, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37449467/): Taking carbohydrate in longer sessions and races is well supported; the benefit for a trained runner in a roughly 1-hour race is smaller.
- **fuel-contraception-coc-masks-energy-deficiency** (consensus, moderate quality; Williams 2026, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41474492/): In a runner on the combined pill, regular bleeds are withdrawal bleeds and say nothing about her energy availability. Look instead at her menstrual history before the pill, bone stress injuries, weight or BMI change, signs of dietary restriction, and why she started the pill, and never present the pill as protecting bone.
- **fuel-iron-daily-supplement-menstruating-women-gi-cost** (meta_analysis, moderate quality; Low 2016, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27087396/): Iron reduces anaemia and iron deficiency, and it has a cost: gut side effects, including constipation and loose stools, were about twice as likely with iron (relative risks; the abstract gives no absolute rates), and in a runner they could disrupt training and races (reviewer inference). Weigh that cost before iron is taken routinely, and leave dosing to her clinician once a deficiency is measured.
- **fuel-iron-female-athlete-deficiency-endurance-cost** (systematic_review, low quality; Pengelly 2025, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39536912/): For a woman in hard endurance training, low iron is a plausible and testable cause of flat endurance performance: suggest a ferritin and haemoglobin test through her clinician rather than guessing. Gains seen within groups at 100 mg doses of elemental iron (daily by mouth or every second day by injection), in athletes shown to be deficient, did not differ significantly from placebo; dose and route are the clinician's call, not the coach's.
- **fuel-iron-ferrous-sulfate-raises-stores-active-women** (meta_analysis, moderate quality; McCarthy 2026, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42271116/): Oral iron raises iron stores in active women, most clearly in trials whose participants started deficient (a subgroup finding), but, as most trials that measured VO2max or lactate found no significant change, it should not be expected to lift performance tests by itself. Treat it as correcting a measured deficiency, under a clinician and with a repeat test, not as a performance supplement.
- **fuel-iron-low-ferritin-half-of-elite-women** (observational, low quality; Nabhan 2020, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31901316/): Low iron stores are common in women athletes; because these women matched general-population women, the share is likely similar below elite level (reviewer inference). A woman runner who has never had ferritin checked has a real chance of low stores, which is a reason to ask about a recent test.
- **fuel-iron-oral-response-depends-on-baseline-ferritin** (meta_analysis, moderate quality; Šmid 2024, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38407751/): Whether oral iron helps depends on how low ferritin is to begin with, which only a blood test shows. Expect weeks, not days, before stores rise, and plan a repeat test rather than an open-ended course; a mildly low ferritin may barely move on oral iron alone, which is for her clinician to manage.
- **fuel-iron-supplement-vo2max-women-reproductive-age** (meta_analysis, low quality; Pasricha 2014, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24717371/): In women, iron supplementation measurably improved aerobic capacity across trials; this supports checking iron in a woman whose aerobic fitness stalls despite consistent training. The abstract does not show a benefit in women whose iron is already normal.
- **fuel-lea-common-in-athletes** (meta_analysis, low quality; Gallant 2025, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39485653/): Low energy availability is common enough to ask about in any committed runner, female or male, rather than waiting for an obvious eating problem. The questionnaire the review names (LEAF-Q) is written for women.
- **fuel-lea-indicators-slower-marathon-women** (observational, low quality; Whitney 2025, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39532316/): In a woman training for a marathon, under-fuelling is common and goes with slower racing and lost training. Screen for it with questions about eating and cycle changes, and do not rule it out on BMI, which did not differ between the groups.
- **fuel-lea-markers-not-obligatory-underperformance** (meta_analysis, low quality; Kuikman 2022, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35819582/): A dip in performance under heavy training is not proof of under-fuelling, and markers of low energy availability over a few weeks do not guarantee a performance loss; weigh load and recovery as well as energy before naming a cause.
- **fuel-low-energy-availability-reds** (consensus, moderate quality; Mountjoy 2023, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37752011/): Treat repeated bone-stress injury, unexplained fatigue, performance decline or illness in a high-volume runner as a prompt to check energy intake, not just training load.
- **fuel-menstrual-disturbance-dose-not-threshold** (consensus, moderate quality; De Souza 2026, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41474493/): Do not try to clear or flag a woman by computing energy availability from food logs and watch calories, which the consensus calls unreliable outside the laboratory. Ask instead about unplanned weight loss and changes in her cycle, and treat a sustained deficit of a few hundred kcal a day as enough to matter.
- **fuel-more-food-restores-menses** (rct, low quality; De Souza 2021, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34164675/): When a runner's cycle has become irregular or stopped and energy deficiency is the likely cause, the tested remedy is a moderate, sustained increase in food intake under medical and dietetic care; expect months, and do not read the first period as full recovery.
- **fuel-race-carbohydrate-scales-with-duration** (narrative_review, low quality; Burke 2019, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30747558/): Plan race fuelling by expected duration: for about an hour, a carbohydrate-rich pre-race meal and little or no in-race intake; for a marathon, practise high hourly intake in training.
- **fuel-train-the-gut** (narrative_review, low quality; Jeukendrup 2017, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28332114/): Rehearse the race fuelling plan, products and amounts, in long runs and race-pace sessions in the weeks before the race.
- **fuel-triad-factors-raise-bone-stress-risk** (consensus, moderate quality; De Souza 2026, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41474493/): For a woman runner who still has a natural cycle, missed or irregular periods are a bone-stress warning as well as an energy one. A bone stress injury, especially a second one or one at the hip, pelvis, sacrum or heel, is a reason to ask about her cycle and eating, and to seek medical review before load rises.
